Mastering the Shuttle Buses

You do not need a car in Hakuba. Five bus systems link the resorts and the main lodging areas. They can be confusing at first, but each one does a different job.

Last season’s fare for the Hakuba Valley Shuttle was ¥800 for adults and ¥400 for children, paid by online ticket. Timetables change in December and again in mid-March, so always check the current version.

How to ride it well

  • Mornings run out, afternoons run home. Most morning buses head toward the resorts and most afternoon buses come back. Plan your return around the last bus, not the last lift.
  • Full means full. On powder days the buses fill up and skip stops once they are full. Be at the stop 10–15 minutes early, or catch it one stop earlier on the route.
  • Carry your pass. If you ride free with a Hakuba Valley pass, have it ready when you board. Without one, buy the online ticket before you get on, because drivers do not take cash.
  • Skis go outside. Most buses have racks or a luggage space. Take off your boots’ buckles and keep your poles together so boarding is quick.
  • Use the app. Hakuba Snow Compass shows which bus systems serve today’s top pick and links to the current official timetable.

Taxis, Rental Cars and Driving on Snow

Taxis are scarce — plan for it. Winter visitor numbers far outstrip the valley’s taxi fleet. Extra cars are brought in from nearby towns each season, but demand still peaks hard from about 7:00 to 9:00 and 16:00 to 20:00.

  • Book dinner rides a day ahead. If you have a restaurant booking, reserve the taxi there and back when you make it. Many restaurants and lodges will call one for you.
  • Use Uber. In winter, Uber works in Hakuba through local taxi companies. It is cashless, you see the fare up front, and you can drop a pin, which helps when you can’t pronounce your lodge’s name.
  • Late at night, a demand-taxi service runs in the evenings in peak season. Ask your lodge for the current number.
  • Carry the address in Japanese. Save your lodge’s name and address in Japanese on your phone and show it to the driver.

Rental cars: freedom, with homework. A car makes it easy to chase snow north to Cortina or soak in onsen on rest days. It also means driving on snow and ice in a country that drives on the left.

  • Paperwork: you need your home licence plus an International Driving Permit under the 1949 Geneva Convention, or an official Japanese translation for some countries. Rental companies will not give you a car without it.
  • Book early. Winter rental fleets in Nagano sell out over Christmas, New Year and February.
  • Tyres and 4WD: rentals in snow country come with winter tyres. Ask for 4WD. Many lodges sit on steep, unploughed side roads.
  • Parking: resorts have large car parks, but some lodges have little space. Ask before you arrive.

Driving on snow: the short version

  1. Double your following distance and brake early and gently.
  2. Slow down before corners, not in them.
  3. Watch for black ice on bridges, in tunnel exits and on shaded roads in the early morning.
  4. Lift your wipers off the glass when you park overnight so they don’t freeze to it.
  5. Snowploughs work at night and early morning. Never park on the road, or your car may be buried or towed.
  6. If conditions look bad, leave the car and take the shuttle. Nobody has missed a powder day by riding the bus.
